\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
In this report we investigate whether the text extracted from a company's 10-K filing can be used to effectively predict the volatility of that company's share price, beyond what we could predict from last year's volatility alone.

Every company listed in the US must file a 10-K with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) each year. It is long (the median report in our data runs to about 12,000 words across the two sections we use) and it is written partly to warn investors about what could go wrong. If that warning carries real information, a model that reads the report should predict risk better than one that doesn't.

In this project we measured risk by volatility, the industry standard for how `risky' a stock is. Being able to accurately predict volatility has critical implications for option pricing (via Black-Scholes; \citealp{black1973}), risk management, regulation and broader portfolio construction. Given the sheer scale of capital that volatility forecasts are used to guide, the cost of running a model like ours is greatly outweighed by the value of even a small improvement in forecast accuracy.

The volatility of a stock is nothing more than the standard deviation of its returns. We formalise this with the notation below:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $p_t$ denotes the closing price on trading day $t$.
  \item $r_t$ is the log return given by
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:logreturn}
    r_t = \log\frac{p_t}{p_{t-1}}.
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
We then define the volatility $\sigma$ by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vol}
  \sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(r_t - \bar{r}\right)^2},
  \qquad \bar{r} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the total number of trading days in a year, typically 252. We then write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logvol}
  y = \log \sigma\text{.}
\end{equation}
Here $y$ is called the log volatility. We use both the log volatility and the log returns as these often play nicer than their standard equivalents. For example, one useful property is the volatilities of companies are right-skewed, applying a log to volatility turns this into a more familiar normal-ish distribution \citep{andersen2001}. They also carry the benefit of allowing us to compare our results directly to the paper this report is inspired by \citep{kogan2009}.

As a rule of thumb, the volatility of a company's returns is relatively consistent each year. This is because `risky' stocks tend to have idiosyncratic properties that persist over time that make them more prone to risk than other stocks. For example the volatility of NVIDIA will almost always be higher than that of PepsiCo. This is because people will always drink Pepsi but AI infrastructure demand remains a hot topic of debate.

With this in mind, predicting the next year's volatility for a company based on just the volatility of previous years is a simple and effective method. We used this as the baseline and investigated how adding information about a company's SEC filings changes prediction accuracy. Following \citet{kogan2009}, we predicted the log of volatility and scored models by their mean squared error (MSE).

We used about 27,000 10-K reports from 1996 to 2006. We started by building a ridge regression, the simplest model we can build that looks at the text. Then we built an attention network and a Multi Layer Perceptron (MLP) in an attempt to beat this baseline. We also experimented with different encoders to determine the effect of different tokenisation techniques on text understanding.

It's worth noting here that even a negative result is informative. If we find that including the text of the 10-K filings does not improve prediction accuracy then this implies something potentially more interesting. It would suggest that the information in the report is already reflected in how the share price has moved, and so is already contained in last year's volatility. This is what one would expect in an efficient market, where the price of a stock should reflect all publicly available information. This is a theme that we will return to throughout the report as many of our models did not yield significant improvements over the baseline. \placeholder{TODO: add numbers once models are trained}

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Choosing the data}\label{sec:data-choice}
When sourcing data for the project, we needed reliable sources for both the 10-K reports and the share price volatility in the year after they were filed. The 10-K reports are easy enough to source; every report is public on EDGAR, the SEC's online filing system. The challenge we faced here was sourcing the volatility labels for the data. This requires the daily prices for every company, including the ones that went bust or were taken over. Leaving out these firms would introduce significant survivorship bias, as we would automatically be left with safer companies.

The obvious strategy was to take the text of the 10-K reports from EDGAR and compute the volatility ourselves from Yahoo Finance prices, downloaded with \texttt{yfinance}, an unofficial Python library. To test the feasibility of this strategy, we applied it to the reports in Kogan's corpus filed in 2004, for which the correct labels are already known. We looked up each company's ticker in the SEC's list of tickers and compared the volatility we computed from Yahoo with Kogan's label for the same report. Where prices existed, the calculations held up -- our values correlated 0.99 with Kogan's. However, only 26\% of the reports (917 of 3,558) could be labelled at all, because the SEC's list only holds tickers for companies that are listed today, and the companies we lost were about 23\% more volatile on average. This is exactly the survivorship bias described above. This ruled out Yahoo as our source of labels \supp{02-Data, \S1}.

To combat this, we instead relied on the 10-K corpus of \citet{kogan2009}, which covers reports filed from 1996 to 2006. For each report it gives the log volatility over the year before filing, which is our baseline input, and over the year after, which is our target. These labels were computed from CRSP (the Center for Research in Security Prices), the standard academic price database, which keeps a company's prices after it is delisted.

We now move on to selecting what items to analyse from the reports themselves. Refereing to Table~\ref{tab:items} we chose to work with only items 1 and 7. Whilst including the entire reports in our analysis is a potential area for further exploration \placeholder{potential link to later section on scalability} we elected to work with just these as they showed the most promise and every extra section costs more time to extract the text.

Kogan's corpus contains only Item 7 of each report, so we took the rest of the text from EDGAR-CORPUS \citep{loukas2021}, a dataset of 10-K sections extracted from EDGAR. From it we took Item 1, and a second copy of Item 7 that we used to check Kogan's extraction (Section~\ref{sec:data-text}). This approach also let us use Kogan's published scores as a benchmark. We also considered Bloomberg, which is available only in person and whose data cannot be shared publicly; WRDS, which provides CRSP but at Bristol is restricted to Business and Economics masters students; and an extension of Kogan's corpus to 2013 by researchers at NCCU, which we could not download \supp{02-Data, \S1}.

\subsection{Building the text}\label{sec:data-text}
Kogan's reports were matched to EDGAR-CORPUS on the company's SEC number (CIK) and the filing year. Kogan's company name appeared in the EDGAR text for 98.9\% of matched reports, with the other cases caused primarily by spelling differences (e.g.\ ``Applebee's'' against ``APPLEBEES''). We found 95 companies that filed two 10-K reports in one calendar year. Since EDGAR-CORPUS identifies a report only by company and year, we could not tell which of the two filings its text belonged to, so we removed both copies of these duplicates. EDGAR-CORPUS is about 14\,GB, so we streamed the data and kept only Items 1 and 7 of the reports for which we were able to confirm a match with Kogan. This brought the size of the dataset down to a few hundred MB, a much more manageable task for our available compute. There were also 1,989 cases where EDGAR-CORPUS had no 10-K at all for that company and year, along with 1,144 cases where we were able to identify a match but Item 1 was too short to be useful (fewer than 100 words). In both cases, we cut these data points.

This left us with 27,142 of the original 30,465 (89\%) Kogan reports, with the drop in any single year never exceeding 13\%. Given the near miss with survivorship bias caused by Yahoo, we again investigated whether the dropped reports had a different risk profile. We ran a Welch t-test on next year's log volatility $y$. Writing $\mu_{\text{kept}}$ and $\mu_{\text{lost}}$ for the mean of $y$ over the kept and lost reports, we tested
\[
H_0:\ \mu_{\text{kept}} = \mu_{\text{lost}}
\qquad \text{against} \qquad
H_1:\ \mu_{\text{kept}} \neq \mu_{\text{lost}}
\]
using the statistic
\[
t = \frac{\bar{y}_{\text{lost}} - \bar{y}_{\text{kept}}}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_{\text{kept}}^2}{n_{\text{kept}}} + \dfrac{s_{\text{lost}}^2}{n_{\text{lost}}}}},
\qquad
\nu = \frac{\left(\dfrac{s_{\text{kept}}^2}{n_{\text{kept}}} + \dfrac{s_{\text{lost}}^2}{n_{\text{lost}}}\right)^2}{\dfrac{\left(s_{\text{kept}}^2/n_{\text{kept}}\right)^2}{n_{\text{kept}}-1} + \dfrac{\left(s_{\text{lost}}^2/n_{\text{lost}}\right)^2}{n_{\text{lost}}-1}},
\]
where $\bar{y}$, $s^2$ and $n$ are each group's sample mean, variance and size, and $t$ is compared with a $t$-distribution on $\nu$ degrees of freedom. Unlike Student's t-test, Welch's test does not assume the two groups have the same variance, which suits groups as unequal in size as ours. We ran the test once for the reports lost because EDGAR-CORPUS had no match, and once for all lost reports. Since $y$ is a logarithm, $100\left(e^{\bar{y}_{\text{lost}} - \bar{y}_{\text{kept}}} - 1\right)$ gives how much more volatile the lost reports were as a percentage (Table~\ref{tab:welch}).

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

Initially, we fitted a straight line on last year's volatility first and then fitted the text to what it got wrong, but in every final model, we fitted last year's volatility and the text together. This is the correct way to measure what the text adds beyond last year's volatility, because fitting them one after the other fixes the weight on last year's volatility before the text is seen. Fitting them together also gave a lower MSE on both windows. Some comparison runs were only done with the text fitted after the straight line (two-step), and these are labelled as such. For every network, once early stopping has chosen the number of epochs, we refit the model on the whole training window, including the last training year.

\subsection{Scoring}\label{sec:methods-scoring}

For each test year, each model predicts the volatility of each company's shares over the 12 months after its report. Each prediction is then checked against the true volatility, and scored by the mean squared error (MSE) on log volatility, as seen in \citet{kogan2009}.

An MSE on its own does not necessarily say whether a model is good or bad. So, to give each model something to beat, we built two baselines that do not use the text at all and scored them on the same test reports. The first is copying last year's volatility, as seen in \citet{kogan2009}. The second is a straight line, which is an ordinary least-squares fit of next year's volatility on last year's volatility, without text. This line uses the same information as every model apart from the text. However, the models also fit their own weight on last year's volatility, so some of their gain over the line could come from this rather than from the text. To check this, we also fitted the within-year slope on last year's volatility with no text. This only lowers the MSE by 0.5\% on the paper window and 0.0\% on the strict window, so nearly all of the models' gains must come from the text. To check whether a difference in MSE is real rather than chance, we used a paired bootstrap over the test reports.

We split each test year's MSE into two parts,
\[
  \text{MSE} = \bar e^{\,2} + \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (e_i - \bar e)^2 .
\]
The first part, the level, is how far off the model is on the whole year's volatility. The second, the spread, is how well the model ranks the companies within the year. This is what the text could help with. For the straight line, the level is 44\% of the MSE in 2003 and 30\% in 2001, so a model can look much better or worse depending on the year.

\subsection{The linear baseline}\label{sec:methods-ridge}

We then fitted a ridge regression on each report's averaged FinBERT vectors. It is fitted at the same time as the weight on last year's volatility, which is not penalised. A ridge model is the simplest model that uses the text, and it is the same as a neural network with no hidden layer. This creates another benchmark that the attention network and MLP would have to beat to prove that they are useful.

We found that fitting all years together didn't help. On Item 7, it lowers the MSE by 5.9\% on the paper window, against 3.8\% within year. However, all of this gain comes from the level in 2003 and 2004. This means that it was using the text to guess the year rather than to tell companies apart. On the strict window, this gain disappears, and the pooled model also ranks companies worse. The final version therefore only uses the text within each year. Ridge regression only needs $X^\top X$ and $X^\top y$, so, following the map/reduce idea from Lecture 09.2 \citep{dst092}, we computed these once per year and summed them for each training window.

Fitting the text and last year's volatility together made a large difference. Fitted jointly, the within-year ridge lowered the MSE by 3.8\% on the paper window and 2.3\% on the strict window, compared to 1.0\% and 0.0\% when the text is fitted after the straight line. This is because part of the text tells us the same thing as last year's volatility. When the line is fitted first, it takes the credit for this shared part, so the text can only help with what is left over. When they are fitted together, the Frisch--Waugh--Lovell theorem shows that the text is judged on the part of it that the volatility of last year cannot explain.

Comparing this model with the paper gave an interesting result. In \citeauthor{kogan2009}'s data and training window, the straight line without text scores 0.1403 for 2006 against 0.1402 for their best model that uses the text. This suggests that the data in the text may not help much. We also found that the 11\% of reports that our data could not match to EDGAR-CORPUS \citep{loukas2021} are the hardest reports to predict, with 3 to 6 times the error of copying, so leaving them out lowers our scores. FETILDA \citep{xia2022} reported a 27\% gain over \citeauthor{kogan2009}'s baseline but measured on a different subset of reports. What we are seeing could be a similar thing happening.

\subsection{The attention network}\label{sec:methods-attention}

Rather than giving every chunk the same weight, the attention model keeps each report's chunk vectors separate and uses attention pooling. It assigns each chunk a score, converts the scores to weights with a softmax, and predicts the within-year volatility from the weighted average of the chunk vectors, with its own unpenalised weight on last year's volatility. For the softmax, the ReLU and tanh layers, and the training loop, we closely followed the lectures and Workshop 06.3 \citep{dst063}, with early stopping, dropout and weight decay.

On Item 7, the attention network lowers the MSE by 3.9\% on the paper window and 1.9\% on the strict window, against 3.8\% and 2.3\% for the ridge. This is level on the paper window and slightly worse on the strict window, but both differences are within the spread between seeds, which is about 1.2 to 1.4 percentage points per year. So, on the ProsusAI vectors, the attention network still does no better than ridge.

To compare different versions of the network, we trained four versions (attention pooling on Item 7, on Item 1 and on both sections, and mean pooling on Item 7), each with 5 seeds, 2 windows and 6 test years, giving 240 runs. These runs were done before the joint fit, so they are two-step. No model variant did much better than the within-year ridge fitted in the same way. Learning the chunk weights also added little, since attention beat mean pooling by only 0.3\%, less than the spread between seeds. The learnt weights are close to equal, with an effective number of chunks 78\% of the actual number. Therefore, attention pooling behaves almost like mean pooling. The model did favour Item 7, placing 78\% of its weight there when given both sections, even though Item 7 makes up only 62\% of the chunks. Item 1 on its own did worse than the straight line. Since none of these versions did much better than attention pooling on Item 7, this was the only version we refitted jointly.

We also refitted the model on the last training year once the number of epochs had been chosen, since early stopping holds that year back. This improved the network by 0.7\% on the paper window and 0.1\% on the strict window.

\subsection{The second encoder}\label{sec:methods-encoder}

After seeing the results of the attention network, we thought that the vectors themselves could be limiting how much the models gain over the no-text benchmarks. Our vectors come from ProsusAI/finbert, which was tuned to judge the sentiment of sentences, so they mostly capture tone. Therefore, we re-encoded Item 7 with the FinBERT of \citet{yang2020}, which was pre-trained on 10-Ks and analyst reports. Then, we reran the same ridge regression and attention network on the new vectors. For each chunk, we kept both the CLS vector and the mean of the token vectors, since, without fine-tuning, the CLS vector is not trained to summarise the chunk.

Yang's vectors beat ProsusAI's by about 0.7 to 1.2 percentage points on the paper window, and by up to 0.7 on the strict window. The ridge model improved to $-4.5\%$ and $-2.6\%$ on Yang's CLS vectors, and the attention network got $-5.1\%$ and $-2.6\%$ on Yang's token averages. So, the new encoder helped, although only modestly. When the text was fit after the straight line, the two encoders gave similar results, so the two-step fit hid this difference. Choosing the CLS or the token-average vector made little difference. For the attention network, they are level on the paper window, and the token average is slightly better on the strict window ($-2.6\%$ against $-2.0\%$).

\section{Discussion and conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{The data}

The first challenge of the project was the labels, not the text. Computing volatility ourselves from Yahoo Finance would only have labelled 26\% of reports, and the reports we lost were about 23\% more volatile, so we used \citeauthor{kogan2009}'s CRSP labels instead. Matching these to EDGAR-CORPUS kept 27,142 of the 30,465 reports (89\%), and neither Welch t-test found a significant difference between the reports we kept and the ones we lost. However, the lost reports are not harmless for scoring. They are the hardest reports to predict, with 3 to 6 times the error of copying last year's volatility, so leaving them out lowers our MSE. This means that our scores cannot be compared directly with \citeauthor{kogan2009}'s, and only the changes against a baseline on the same reports should be compared. This is why every model in this report is scored against the straight line on exactly the same test reports.

\subsection{What made the difference}

The choice of model made very little difference. Once last year's volatility and the text were fitted together, and the text was only used within each year, the ridge regression, the attention network and the MLP all lowered the MSE by about a similar amount. The differences in accuracy between the models are within the spread between seeds, which is about 1.2 to 1.4 percentage points per year. So, the hidden layers of the networks did not find anything in the vectors that the ridge model could not.

What did make a difference was how the text was fitted and how it was turned into numbers. Fitting the text after the straight line made it seem as though the text did not help, with the ridge model only getting $-1.0\%$ and $0.0\%$, against $-3.8\%$ and $-2.3\%$ when fitted jointly. Another difference arose from fitting all years together. This looked better on the paper window, but the extra gain came from guessing the level of the whole year's volatility, and disappeared on the strict window. Within year, FinBERT's vectors lowered the MSE by about 4\% on the paper window, against $-0.2\%$ to $-2.4\%$ for word counts, and the FinBERT of \citet{yang2020}, pre-trained on 10-Ks, did better again at up to $-5.1\%$. So, the representation of the text matters much more than the model built on top of it.

\subsection{Cost and scalability}

Almost all of the computational time is spent reading the text. Reading our 27,142 reports took 12 hours on a laptop, and would have taken around two hours on a Colab T4. In comparison, one epoch of the attention network on the saved vectors takes about six seconds. The cost grows proportionally to the amount of text, so 1000 times more data would take about 70 days on a T4. However, every chunk is read on its own, so the reading step is a map with a trivial reduce, and splits well across many GPUs. On 1,024 T4s, it would take approximately two hours, so this is scalable. Since the ridge model does as well as the networks, we would choose it as the model to use. It is also cheap to scale, since it only needs $X^\top X$ and $X^\top y$ from each year, which can be computed separately and then summed, in the same way as the reading step.

\subsection{What this means financially}

A 4 to 5\% fall in MSE sounds small, but volatility forecasts are used to guide very large amounts of capital. On the paper window, the typical error of the straight line is about 43\% of the true volatility, and our best model brings this down to about 41\%. Since the price of an at-the-money option moves roughly in proportion to volatility, this is also roughly the improvement in pricing for such an option.

It is just as important to know where the gain comes from. Most of it comes from ranking companies within each year, and little from predicting the level of the whole market's volatility. So, the text tells us which companies will be riskier than others in the same year, but not whether next year will be a volatile year. This makes it most useful for comparing companies, for example in portfolio construction or relative-value trading, rather than for timing the market. It also fits with what we would expect in an efficient market. Most of what can be predicted is already in last year's volatility, and the text only adds a little on top. We saw the same thing on \citeauthor{kogan2009}'s own data, where their best text model scores 0.1402 in 2006, almost exactly the same as the 0.1403 of the straight line with no text.

\subsection{Limitations and next steps}

Our data covers 1996 to 2006, so it does not include the financial crisis, and we do not know whether the text would help more or less in a crisis. Item 1A (Risk Factors) only became mandatory in late 2005, so we could not use it, even though it is the section most directly about risk. We also left out the 11\% of reports that we could not match, which are the hardest to predict. Our encoders were frozen in every model except the fine-tuned one, which was only run on one test year with one seed. Finally, many of the differences between models are smaller than the spread between seeds, so we cannot rank models that are within about a point of each other.

There are three clear next steps. Firstly, we would extend the data past 2006. This would let us include Item 1A and the financial crisis. Secondly, we would fine-tune the encoder jointly on more test years and seeds \placeholder{update once 05b is done}. Finally, we would add other text, such as 8-K filings or earnings-call transcripts. This would give the models more to work with, and would also test our scaling estimates.

\subsection{Does the text help?}

Yes, but only a little, and only when it is used in the right way. This means fitting the text and last year's volatility together, and only using the text within each year. When we do this, the text lowers the MSE by around 4\% to 5\% on the paper window and 2\% to 2.6\% on the strict window, compared to the straight line. All of this comes from ranking companies within each year. The model used made little difference, but how the text was represented did. FinBERT clearly beat word counts, and the FinBERT pre-trained on 10-Ks beat the one tuned for sentiment.

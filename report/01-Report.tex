\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
In this report we investigate whether the text extracted from a company's 10-K filing can be used to effectively predict the volatility of that company's share price, beyond what we could predict from last year's volatility alone.

Every company listed in the US must file a 10-K with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) each year. It is long (the median report in our data runs to about 12,000 words across the two sections we use) and it is written partly to warn investors about what could go wrong. If that warning carries real information, a model that reads the report should predict risk better than one that doesn't.

In this project we measured risk by volatility, the industry standard for how `risky' a stock is. Being able to accurately predict volatility has critical implications for option pricing (via Black-Scholes; \citealp{black1973}), risk management, regulation and broader portfolio construction. Given the sheer scale of capital that volatility forecasts are used to guide, the cost of running a model like ours is greatly outweighed by the value of even a small improvement in forecast accuracy.

The volatility of a stock is nothing more than the standard deviation of its returns. We formalise this with the notation below:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $p_t$ denotes the closing price on trading day $t$.
  \item $r_t$ is the log return given by
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:logreturn}
    r_t = \log\frac{p_t}{p_{t-1}}.
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
We then define the volatility $\sigma$ by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vol}
  \sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(r_t - \bar{r}\right)^2},
  \qquad \bar{r} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the total number of trading days in a year, typically 252. We then write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logvol}
  y = \log \sigma\text{.}
\end{equation}
Here $y$ is called the log volatility. We use both the log volatility and the log returns as these are often better behaved than their standard equivalents. For example, the volatilities of companies are right-skewed, and taking the log turns this into a distribution much closer to normal \citep{andersen2001}. They also carry the benefit of allowing us to compare our results directly to the paper this report is inspired by \citep{kogan2009}.

As a rule of thumb, the volatility of a company's returns is relatively consistent each year \citep{engle1982}. This is because `risky' stocks tend to have idiosyncratic properties that persist over time that make them more prone to risk than other stocks. For example, the volatility of NVIDIA will almost always be higher than that of PepsiCo. This is because people will always drink Pepsi but AI infrastructure demand remains a hot topic of debate.

With this in mind, predicting the next year's volatility for a company based on just the volatility of previous years is a simple and effective method. We used this as the baseline and investigated how adding information about a company's SEC filings changes prediction accuracy. Following \citet{kogan2009}, we predicted the log of volatility and scored models by their mean squared error (MSE).

We used about 27,000 10-K reports from 1996 to 2006. We started by building a ridge regression, the simplest model we can build that looks at the text. Then we built an attention network and a multilayer perceptron (MLP) in an attempt to beat this baseline. We also experimented with different encoders to determine the effect of the encoder's pre-training on text understanding.

It's worth noting here that even a negative result is informative. If we find that including the text of the 10-K filings does not improve prediction accuracy then this implies something potentially more interesting. It would suggest that the information in the report is already reflected in how the share price has moved, and so is already contained in last year's volatility. This is what one would expect in an efficient market, where the price of a stock should reflect all publicly available information \citep{fama1970}. This is a theme that we will return to throughout the report as the text improved on the baseline only slightly, even in our best models. \placeholder{TODO: add numbers once models are trained}

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Choosing the data}\label{sec:data-choice}
When sourcing data for the project, we needed reliable sources for both the 10-K reports and the share price volatility in the year after they were filed. The 10-K reports are easy enough to source; every report is public on EDGAR, the SEC's online filing system. The challenge we faced here was sourcing the volatility labels for the data. This requires the daily prices for every company, including the ones that went bust or were taken over. Leaving out these firms would introduce significant survivorship bias, as we would automatically be left with safer companies.

The obvious strategy was to take the text of the 10-K reports from EDGAR and compute the volatility ourselves from Yahoo Finance prices, downloaded with \texttt{yfinance}, an unofficial Python library. To test the feasibility of this strategy, we used the corpus of \citet{kogan2009}, a published dataset of 10-K reports from 1996 to 2006 that already comes with volatility labels. We applied our strategy to its reports filed in 2004 and compared the volatility we computed from Yahoo with Kogan's label for the same report, looking up each company's ticker in the SEC's list of tickers. Where prices existed, the calculations held up---our values correlated 0.99 with Kogan's. However, only 26\% of the reports (917 of 3,558) could be labelled at all, because the SEC's list only holds tickers for companies that are listed today, and the companies we lost were about 23\% more volatile on average. This is exactly the survivorship bias described above. This ruled out Yahoo as our source of labels \supp{02-Data, \S1}.

To combat this, we instead relied on Kogan's labels themselves. For each report the corpus gives the log volatility over the year before filing, which is our baseline input, and over the year after, which is our target. These labels were computed from CRSP (the Center for Research in Security Prices), the standard academic price database, which keeps a company's prices after it is delisted.

We now move on to selecting what items to analyse from the reports themselves. Referring to Table~\ref{tab:items}, we chose to work with only Items 1 and 7. Whilst including the entire reports in our analysis is a potential area for further exploration (Section~\ref{sec:scaling}), we elected to work with just these as they showed the most promise and every extra section costs more time to extract the text.

Kogan's corpus contains only Item 7 of each report, so we took the rest of the text from EDGAR-CORPUS \citep{loukas2021}, a dataset of 10-K sections extracted from EDGAR. From it we took Item 1, and a second copy of Item 7 that we used to check Kogan's extraction (Section~\ref{sec:data-text}). This approach also let us use Kogan's published scores as a benchmark. We also considered Bloomberg, which is available only in person and whose data cannot be shared publicly; WRDS, which provides CRSP but at Bristol is restricted to Business and Economics master's students; and an extension of Kogan's corpus to 2013 by researchers at NCCU, which we could not download \supp{02-Data, \S1}.

\subsection{Building the text}\label{sec:data-text}
Kogan's reports were matched to EDGAR-CORPUS on the company's SEC number (CIK) and the filing year. Kogan's company name appeared in the EDGAR text for 98.9\% of matched reports, with the other cases caused primarily by spelling differences (e.g.\ ``Applebee's'' against ``APPLEBEES''). We found 95 cases where a company filed two 10-K reports in one calendar year. Since EDGAR-CORPUS identifies a report only by company and year, we could not tell which of the two filings its text belonged to, so we removed both copies of these duplicates. EDGAR-CORPUS is about 14\,GB, so we streamed the data and kept only Items 1 and 7 of the reports for which we were able to confirm a match with Kogan. This brought the size of the dataset down to a few hundred MB, which was much more manageable with our available compute. There were also 1,989 cases where EDGAR-CORPUS had no 10-K at all for that company and year, along with 1,144 cases where we were able to identify a match but Item 1 was too short to be useful (fewer than 100 words). In both cases, we cut these data points.

This left us with 27,142 of the original 30,465 (89\%) Kogan reports, with the drop in any single year never exceeding 13\%. Given the near miss with survivorship bias caused by Yahoo, we again investigated whether the dropped reports had a different risk profile. We ran a Welch t-test on next year's log volatility $y$. Writing $\mu_{\text{kept}}$ and $\mu_{\text{lost}}$ for the mean of $y$ over the kept and lost reports, we tested
\[
H_0:\ \mu_{\text{kept}} = \mu_{\text{lost}}
\qquad \text{against} \qquad
H_1:\ \mu_{\text{kept}} \neq \mu_{\text{lost}}.
\]
Unlike Student's t-test, Welch's test \citep{welch1947} does not assume the two groups have the same variance, which suits groups as unequal in size as ours. We ran the test once for the reports lost because EDGAR-CORPUS had no match, and once for all lost reports. Since $y$ is a logarithm, $100\left(e^{\bar{y}_{\text{lost}} - \bar{y}_{\text{kept}}} - 1\right)$, where $\bar{y}$ is each group's sample mean, gives how much more volatile the lost reports were as a percentage (Table~\ref{tab:welch}).

The one thing that remained was to choose which source to use for Item 7 of the report. Recall that we had extracts from both Kogan and EDGAR and that these were extracted with different software, Kogan's header-matching script and EDGAR-CORPUS's parser. We found that the two sources mostly agreed: the median of the length ratio (EDGAR/Kogan) was 0.94 and 88\% of reports fell within 0.5--2$\times$. The exceptions were that EDGAR was much shorter for 9.2\% and much longer for 2.4\%. We investigated some examples of both cases. We identified the following (Table~\ref{tab:item7}):

Thus we decided on the following strategy:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item FinBERT read EDGAR's Item 7 by default because it is clean text with headings (like FinBERT's training text) and comes from the same parser as Item 1.
  \item We fell back to Kogan's copy when EDGAR's had under 100 words, which occurred in 2.2\% of reports.
  \item We kept EDGAR's copy even when it ran past the end of Item 7, since this affected at most 2.4\% of reports and added extra text rather than losing any.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The volatility labels}\label{sec:data-labels}
Since every one of our models is trained to predict Kogan's labels, it was important to perform a few quick sense checks. Recall from the Introduction that the label $y$ is the log of the standard deviation of daily log returns over the 12 months after filing, with the same quantity over the 12 months before filing used as our baseline input. We first checked that the labels were complete and consistent. Every report had a before label and an after label. The labels in our final dataset matched Kogan's raw files for 100\% of reports, and there were no missing or infinite values.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth, trim={0 0 0 26bp}, clip]{fig3_labels.png}
  \caption{The volatility labels. (a) Log volatility in the 12 months after filing against the 12 months before; the dashed line marks no change. (b) Log volatility after filing across all reports, with the median company and the median of the S\&P 500 over the same 12 months.}\label{fig:labels}
\end{figure}

Next we checked that the labels looked sensible (Figure~\ref{fig:labels}). Figure~\ref{fig:labels}(a) plots each report's volatility after filing against its volatility before. The points lie close to the diagonal, with a correlation of 0.83, which is exactly why last year's volatility is such a strong baseline and why beating it is hard. Figure~\ref{fig:labels}(b) compares the companies with the market as a whole. Here, and in Table~\ref{tab:extremes} below, we quote volatility per year rather than per day, as is standard in finance. Since daily returns are close to independent, their variances add up over the year, so the annual volatility is
\[
\sigma_{\text{annual}} = \sqrt{252}\,\sigma \approx 15.9\,\sigma.
\]
The median company had a volatility of 50\% a year, against 17\% for the S\&P 500 over the same 12 months, so a typical company was about three times as volatile as the index. This is what we would expect, since a single firm's own news does not average out in the way it does across 500 companies \citep{campbell2001}.

Both tails of Figure~\ref{fig:labels}(b) stretch a long way, so we looked at the extreme reports individually. We defined these as
\[
\text{very low: } y < -5, \qquad \text{very high: } y > -1.5,
\]
which correspond to a daily volatility $\sigma$ below $e^{-5} \approx 0.7\%$ (calmer than the S\&P 500, whose median is about 1.1\% a day) and above $e^{-1.5} \approx 22\%$, where a single day's move of 20\% was typical. In annual terms, these thresholds are about 11\% and 350\% a year. We searched each report's text for takeover wording (e.g.\ ``agreement and plan of merger'', ``tender offer'') and distress wording (e.g.\ ``going concern'', ``Chapter 11''), and checked whether the company filed another 10-K the following year (Table~\ref{tab:extremes}).

The very low reports turned out to be takeovers. Once a company has agreed to be bought for a fixed cash price, its shares trade just below that price until the deal closes, so its measured volatility collapses. 69\% of these reports contain takeover wording, compared with 17\% of ordinary reports, and only 4\% of the companies filed again the following year. Most describe the company's own sale, for example DEKALB to Monsanto and Odwalla to Coca-Cola. Importantly, these companies were not risk-free; they were about to be delisted. The very high reports were the opposite case of companies being in distress. 82\% contain distress wording, such as a going-concern warning from the auditors or a Chapter 11 filing (e.g.\ Salon Media and eMagin). Unlike the takeovers, these companies were already very volatile the year before (a median of 158\%).

Since these are genuine prices rather than data errors, we kept them. However, they matter far more than their numbers suggest. The 70 very low reports make up only 0.26\% of the data but carry 10\% of the baseline's squared error, and removing them alone would cut the baseline MSE from 0.128 to 0.116. They are also where the text has the clearest chance of beating the baseline, since a report announcing ``an agreement and plan of merger'' says something about next year that we would not otherwise have known from just last year's volatility \supp{02-Data, \S4--5}.

\subsection{From text to model input, and the training and test years}\label{sec:data-input}
The last step was to transform our large blocks of text into something a model can learn from. For this we used FinBERT \citep{araci2019}, a version of BERT \citep{devlin2019} trained further on financial text. We did not change FinBERT itself; we only used it to read the reports, and the models in Section~\ref{sec:methods} were trained on what it produced. This is a good use case for transfer learning: FinBERT has already learnt from far more text than our 27,142 reports, so we reuse what it knows rather than learning the language from scratch.

FinBERT can only read 512 tokens at a time, where a token is a word or a piece of a word, and our reports are much longer than this. So for each report we treated Item 1 and Item 7 separately and did the following:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Split the text into FinBERT's own tokens.
  \item Cut the tokens into consecutive chunks of 510, since two of the 512 slots are taken by FinBERT's start and end markers.
  \item Kept at most 64 chunks per section, recording the true number.
  \item Passed each chunk through FinBERT and kept its CLS vector, the 768 numbers FinBERT uses to summarise the whole chunk.
\end{enumerate}

A typical report gave 32 chunks in total, and fewer than 1\% of reports were cut at 64 chunks, so the cap lost very little text (Table~\ref{tab:chunks}). We saved the vectors at half precision (float16), which halves the storage to about 1.4\,GB for all 943,001 chunks. Reading every report took FinBERT about 12 hours on a laptop GPU, so we did this once and every model then reused the saved vectors \supp{02-Data, \S6}. The ridge regression averages a report's chunks into a single vector, while the attention network keeps them separate and learns how much weight to give each one (Section~\ref{sec:methods}).

The reports also changed over the decade. The median length of Item 7 roughly doubled in this time, with the steepest rise around the Sarbanes--Oxley Act of 2002, which tightened reporting standards, and \citet{kogan2009} note the same jump.

This matters for how we split the data into training and test sets. Volatility moves with the market as a whole---it was high around the 2000--2002 crash and low by 2005. If we split the reports at random, a model could learn each year's market sentiment from that year's own reports, and look better than it really is. So, following \citet{kogan2009}, we split by year. Every model was trained only on earlier years and tested on one year at a time, from 2001 to 2006, apart from the fine-tuned FinBERT, which was tested on 2006 only. This also lets us compare our scores directly with the paper's.

However, there is one subtlety. A report filed in December 2005 has a label covering December 2005 to December 2006, which overlaps the 2006 test year. Training on it leaks a little information about 2006's market into the model. We therefore used two training windows (Table~\ref{tab:splits}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item Paper: the five years before the test year, as in \citet{kogan2009}, for direct comparison.
  \item Strict: the four years ending two years before the test year, so that no training label overlaps the test period.
\end{itemize}

The strict window trains on about a fifth fewer reports, and the training years end further from the test year, so we expect every model to score a little worse on it. If a model's gain over the baseline holds up on the strict window, we can be more confident it comes from the text rather than from leaked information about the test year's market. Within each window, we tuned hyperparameters on the last training year and never on the test year. Every model read its splits from the same file, so all of them were scored on exactly the same reports \supp{02-Data, \S7}.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

Initially, we fitted a straight line on last year's volatility first and then fitted the text to what it got wrong, but in every final model, we fitted last year's volatility and the text together. This is the correct way to measure what the text adds beyond last year's volatility, because fitting them one after the other fixes the weight on last year's volatility before the text is seen. Fitting them together also gave a lower MSE on both windows. Some comparison runs were only done with the text fitted after the straight line (two-step), and these are labelled as such. For every network, once early stopping has chosen the number of epochs, we refit the model on the whole training window, including the last training year.

\subsection{Scoring}\label{sec:methods-scoring}

For each test year, each model predicts the volatility of each company's shares over the 12 months after its report. Each prediction is then checked against the true volatility, and scored by the mean squared error (MSE) on log volatility, as seen in \citet{kogan2009}.

An MSE on its own does not necessarily say whether a model is good or bad. So, to give each model something to beat, we built two baselines that do not use the text at all and scored them on the same test reports. The first is copying last year's volatility, as seen in \citet{kogan2009}. The second is a straight line, which is an ordinary least-squares fit of next year's volatility on last year's volatility, without text. This line uses the same information as every model apart from the text. However, the models also fit their own weight on last year's volatility, so some of their gain over the line could come from this rather than from the text. To check this, we also fitted the within-year slope on last year's volatility with no text. This only lowers the MSE by 0.5\% on the paper window and 0.0\% on the strict window, so nearly all of the models' gains must come from the text. To check whether a difference in MSE is real rather than chance, we used a paired bootstrap over the test reports.

We split each test year's MSE into two parts,
\[
  \text{MSE} = \bar e^{\,2} + \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (e_i - \bar e)^2 .
\]
The first part, the level, is how far off the model is on the whole year's volatility. The second, the spread, is how well the model ranks the companies within the year. This is what the text could help with. For the straight line, the level is 44\% of the MSE in 2003 and 30\% in 2001, so a model can look much better or worse depending on the year.

\subsection{The linear baseline}\label{sec:methods-ridge}

We then fitted a ridge regression on each report's averaged FinBERT vectors. It is fitted at the same time as the weight on last year's volatility, which is not penalised. A ridge model is the simplest model that uses the text, and it is the same as a neural network with no hidden layer. This creates another benchmark that the attention network and MLP would have to beat to prove that they are useful.

We found that fitting all years together didn't help. On Item 7, it lowers the MSE by 5.9\% on the paper window, against 3.8\% within year. However, all of this gain comes from the level in 2003 and 2004. This means that it was using the text to guess the year rather than to tell companies apart. On the strict window, this gain disappears, and the pooled model also ranks companies worse. The final version therefore only uses the text within each year. Ridge regression only needs $X^\top X$ and $X^\top y$, so, following the map/reduce idea from Lecture 09.2 \citep{dst092}, we computed these once per year and summed them for each training window.

Fitting the text and last year's volatility together made a large difference. Fitted jointly, the within-year ridge lowered the MSE by 3.8\% on the paper window and 2.3\% on the strict window, compared to 1.0\% and 0.0\% when the text is fitted after the straight line. This is because part of the text tells us the same thing as last year's volatility. When the line is fitted first, it takes the credit for this shared part, so the text can only help with what is left over. When they are fitted together, the Frisch--Waugh--Lovell theorem shows that the text is judged on the part of it that the volatility of last year cannot explain.

Comparing this model with the paper gave an interesting result. In \citeauthor{kogan2009}'s data and training window, the straight line without text scores 0.1403 for 2006 against 0.1402 for their best model that uses the text. This suggests that the data in the text may not help much. We also found that the 11\% of reports that our data could not match to EDGAR-CORPUS \citep{loukas2021} are the hardest reports to predict, with 3 to 6 times the error of copying, so leaving them out lowers our scores. FETILDA \citep{xia2022} reported a 27\% gain over \citeauthor{kogan2009}'s baseline but measured on a different subset of reports. What we are seeing could be a similar thing happening.

\subsection{The attention network}\label{sec:methods-attention}

Rather than giving every chunk the same weight, the attention model keeps each report's chunk vectors separate and uses attention pooling. It assigns each chunk a score, converts the scores to weights with a softmax, and predicts the within-year volatility from the weighted average of the chunk vectors, with its own unpenalised weight on last year's volatility. For the softmax, the ReLU and tanh layers, and the training loop, we closely followed the lectures and Workshop 06.3 \citep{dst063}, with early stopping, dropout and weight decay.

On Item 7, the attention network lowers the MSE by 3.9\% on the paper window and 1.9\% on the strict window, against 3.8\% and 2.3\% for the ridge. This is level on the paper window and slightly worse on the strict window, but both differences are within the spread between seeds, which is about 1.2 to 1.4 percentage points per year. So, on the ProsusAI vectors, the attention network still does no better than ridge.

To compare different versions of the network, we trained four versions (attention pooling on Item 7, on Item 1 and on both sections, and mean pooling on Item 7), each with 5 seeds, 2 windows and 6 test years, giving 240 runs. These runs were done before the joint fit, so they are two-step. No model variant did much better than the within-year ridge fitted in the same way. Learning the chunk weights also added little, since attention beat mean pooling by only 0.3\%, less than the spread between seeds. The learnt weights are close to equal, with an effective number of chunks 78\% of the actual number. Therefore, attention pooling behaves almost like mean pooling. The model did favour Item 7, placing 78\% of its weight there when given both sections, even though Item 7 makes up only 62\% of the chunks. Item 1 on its own did worse than the straight line. Since none of these versions did much better than attention pooling on Item 7, this was the only version we refitted jointly.

We also refitted the model on the last training year once the number of epochs had been chosen, since early stopping holds that year back. This improved the network by 0.7\% on the paper window and 0.1\% on the strict window.

\subsection{The second encoder}\label{sec:methods-encoder}

After seeing the results of the attention network, we thought that the vectors themselves could be limiting how much the models gain over the no-text benchmarks. Our vectors come from ProsusAI/finbert, which was tuned to judge the sentiment of sentences, so they mostly capture tone. Therefore, we re-encoded Item 7 with the FinBERT of \citet{yang2020}, which was pre-trained on 10-Ks and analyst reports. Then, we reran the same ridge regression and attention network on the new vectors. For each chunk, we kept both the CLS vector and the mean of the token vectors, since, without fine-tuning, the CLS vector is not trained to summarise the chunk.

Yang's vectors beat ProsusAI's by about 0.7 to 1.2 percentage points on the paper window, and by up to 0.7 on the strict window. The ridge model improved to $-4.5\%$ and $-2.6\%$ on Yang's CLS vectors, and the attention network got $-5.1\%$ and $-2.6\%$ on Yang's token averages. So, the new encoder helped, although only modestly. When the text was fit after the straight line, the two encoders gave similar results, so the two-step fit hid this difference. Choosing the CLS or the token-average vector made little difference. For the attention network, they are level on the paper window, and the token average is slightly better on the strict window ($-2.6\%$ against $-2.0\%$).

\section{Computational cost and scaling}\label{sec:scaling}

Almost all of our computation went into one step, FinBERT reading the text. FinBERT is a 110-million-parameter network (the BERT-base architecture of \citet{devlin2019}), and it had to read all 943,001 chunks. The attention network, on the other hand, only works on the saved 768-number summaries, and one epoch of it took about six seconds on a Colab T4 GPU. So, when we ask how our approach scales, we are really asking how the reading step scales. We investigated this by timing FinBERT on a laptop's CPU, its GPU (an Apple M3) and a Colab T4, at different precisions and batch sizes \supp{04-Scaling}.

  \small

    Laptop GPU (M3) & fp32 & 22 & -- \\

The T4 was about 6 times faster than the laptop's GPU, which in turn was only 2--3 times faster than its CPU (Table~\ref{tab:speed}). Using half precision (fp16) instead of 32-bit numbers sped the laptop up by about 19\% and halved the size of the saved output, since 16-bit numbers take half the memory and GPUs can process them faster \citep{micikevicius2018}. A check on 50 reports found no measurable change to the vectors. We also found that batch size barely mattered on either GPU. One 510-token chunk is already enough work to keep the GPUs busy, so feeding them more chunks at once adds nothing. We also timed FinBERT on 32 up to 1,024 chunks and found that the time grows linearly with the number of chunks ($R^2 = 0.997$), with no measurable start-up cost. FinBERT therefore costs a fixed price per chunk.

Both full runs were slower than their speed tests, for different reasons. The laptop ran at its speed-test rate for the first hour and a half, then dropped by about 15\% and stayed there for the remaining ten hours. This is likely due to thermal throttling, although we did not record the temperature to confirm it. The T4 ran at a steady rate throughout, so heat was not the issue. Instead, for each batch of reports, Colab first read the text and split it into tokens on its CPU, and only then sent it to the GPU, so the GPU sat idle while the CPU worked. If the GPU alone reads $g$ chunks per second and the CPU step prepares $p$, then done one after the other the rate is
\[
  \text{rate} = \frac{1}{1/g + 1/p}.
\]
With $g = 158$ and the measured rate of 118, this gives $p \approx 460$ chunks per second. This matters for scaling, because the CPU step does not get faster when the GPU does, so the faster the GPU, the larger the share of time it spends waiting. This is Amdahl's law \citep{amdahl1967,dst091}. There are two simple fixes. The first is to run the CPU step alongside the GPU, as PyTorch's \texttt{DataLoader} does, which raises the rate to $\min(g, p)$. The second is to tokenise the text once in advance and store the token ids instead of the raw text.

Next, we looked at how far our data could realistically grow. Our corpus is only a part of what exists. Counting the SEC's own index of every filing, 249,368 10-Ks were filed from 1996 to 2025, and in 2002--2006 Kogan's corpus covered only about a third of them. This is because it only keeps companies whose shares CRSP prices and whose Item 7 Kogan's script could find. We also measured how long recent reports are, using a sample of EDGAR-CORPUS \citep{loukas2021} for 2006, 2013 and 2020. Items 1 and 7 barely grew, from a mean of about 11,600 words to 13,600, while the whole 10-K grew from about 30,800 words to 43,900, mostly because the Risk Factors section (Item 1A) tripled in length. Since FinBERT costs a fixed price per chunk, the cost of each larger dataset follows directly from its number of chunks (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}) \supp{04-Scaling, \S6}.

Extending our companies up to today, adding every company that files a 10-K, and even reading every section of every report all run on a single GPU in under three days. So for any realistic version of this study, compute is not the bottleneck. The real limit is the labels. Every report needs a volatility label, which means daily prices for every company, including the ones that were later delisted. Kogan's labels stop in 2006, and the only source that keeps delisted companies is CRSP, which at Bristol is only available through WRDS to Business and Economics master's students. As we saw in Section~\ref{sec:data-choice}, Yahoo Finance would have labelled only 26\% of the reports and demonstrated considerable survivorship bias. Not every filer has traded shares either (for example funds, trusts and companies that have only issued debt), so ``every 10-K filer'' is an upper bound on what could ever be labelled. With access to CRSP though, repeating our study on every company up to today using the same two items would take about a day on one GPU (22 hours in Table~\ref{tab:ladder}).

The brief also asks how our approach would cope with 1000 times more data. This is about 27 million reports, far more 10-Ks than have ever been filed, so it would mean adding other text, such as quarterly 10-Q reports, 8-K filings, earnings-call transcripts and news. Since the cost is linear, this would take about 93 days on one T4, and produce about 1.45\,TB of FinBERT output. However, each chunk is read independently of every other, so the work splits cleanly across GPUs. In MapReduce terms \citep{dst092}, reading is the map, and the only reduce is collecting each report's vectors back together, which costs almost nothing. With $N$ GPUs,
\[
  \text{time}(N) = \text{start up} + \frac{\text{input size}}{\text{bandwidth}} + \frac{\text{chunks}}{N \times \text{rate per GPU}},
\]
where the first two terms do not shrink as GPUs are added. Assuming the input comes from one shared store at 1\,GB/s, 1,024 T4s, each running at its speed-test rate of 158 chunks per second, would read 1000 times our data in about 2.3 hours, at 71\% of perfect efficiency, or 1.9 hours and 86\% if they read pre-tokenised ids. Beyond this point the shared store becomes the limit, and the fix is to split the data as well as the work, with each worker reading its own shard from its own disk, as Spark or MapReduce do \citep{dean2004}.

Training the models that use the saved vectors would not need a cluster. Even at 1000 times the data, one epoch of the attention network would take about 1.6 hours on a single T4. The ridge regression is cheaper still, since it only needs the sums $X^\top X$ and $X^\top y$, which can be computed separately for each year (or each shard of reports) and then added together, another map with a simple reduce. The word count models scale in a similar way. Their word count matrix takes 0.19\,GB stored sparsely, which would be about 190\,GB at 1000 times the data. This is too big to hold in memory, but it is easy to split into shards. Counting the words took 91 seconds on one CPU core, which would be about 25 hours at 1000 times, or under half an hour on 64 cores.

Fine-tuning is the exception. Here FinBERT itself is trained, so every chunk has to go through the network forwards and backwards on every step. On a T4 this ran at about 40 chunks per second, a quarter of the speed of only reading the text. Even reading just 4 random chunks of each report per step, a single test year with a single seed took about 1.4 GPU-hours, against about 15 minutes for all 60 runs of the attention network on Yang's saved vectors. At 1000 times the data, one epoch would be about 45 million training chunks, or about 310 T4-hours. Unlike reading, the training steps are not independent, since every step updates the same 110 million weights. The work can still be split by giving each GPU part of every batch (data parallelism), but after every step the gradients have to be added up across all the GPUs and sent back. This exchange takes longer the more GPUs there are \citep{dst092}, so each extra GPU speeds up fine-tuning by less than it would speed up reading. Since in our one test it did not clearly beat the models on frozen vectors (Section~\ref{sec:methods}), it is hard to justify at this scale.

These estimates have clear limits. We tested only one laptop and one type of cloud GPU, and the T4 figures come from Yang's FinBERT rather than ours. The storage bandwidth is assumed rather than measured, and fine-tuning was timed on one test year and one seed. The growth after 2006 assumes we keep the same share of filers, and is based on a sample of just three years. Finally, the larger figures are extrapolations, since we only measured the cost per chunk up to about a million chunks.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{What made the difference}

The choice of model made very little difference. Once last year's volatility and the text were fitted together, and the text was only used within each year, the ridge regression, the attention network and the MLP all lowered the MSE by about a similar amount. The differences in accuracy between the models are within the spread between seeds, which is about $1.2$ to $1.4\%$ per year. So, the hidden layers of the networks did not find anything in the vectors that the ridge model could not.

What did make a difference was how the text was fitted and how it was turned into numbers. Fitting the text after the straight line made it seem as though the text did not help, with the ridge model only getting $-1.0\%$ and $0.0\%$, against $-3.8\%$ and $-2.3\%$ when fitted jointly. Another difference arose from fitting all years together. This looked better on the paper window, but the extra gain came from guessing the level of the whole year's volatility, and disappeared on the strict window. Within year, FinBERT's vectors lowered the MSE by about 4\% on the paper window, against $-0.2\%$ to $-2.4\%$ for word counts, and the FinBERT of \citet{yang2020}, pre-trained on 10-Ks, did better again at up to $-5.1\%$. So, the representation of the text matters much more than the model built on top of it.

\subsection{What this means financially}

A 4 to 5\% fall in MSE sounds small, but volatility forecasts are used to guide very large amounts of capital. On the paper window, the typical error of the straight line is about 43\% of the true volatility, and our best model brings this down to about 41\%. Since the price of an at-the-money option moves roughly in proportion to volatility, this is also roughly the improvement in pricing for such an option.

It is just as important to know where the gain comes from. Most of it comes from ranking companies within each year, and little from predicting the level of the whole market's volatility. So, the text tells us which companies will be riskier than others in the same year, but not whether next year will be a volatile year. This makes it most useful for comparing companies, for example in portfolio construction or relative-value trading, rather than for timing the market. It also fits with what we would expect in an efficient market. Most of what can be predicted is already in last year's volatility, and the text only adds a little on top. We saw the same thing on \citeauthor{kogan2009}'s own data, where their best text model scores 0.1402 in 2006, almost exactly the same as the 0.1403 of the straight line with no text.

\subsection{Limitations and next steps}

Our data covers 1996 to 2006, so it does not include the financial crisis, and we do not know whether the text would help more or less in a crisis. Item 1A (Risk Factors) only became mandatory in late 2005, so we could not use it, even though it is the section most directly about risk. We also left out the 11\% of reports that we could not match, which are the hardest to predict. Our encoders were frozen in every model except the fine-tuned one, which was only run on one test year with one seed. Finally, many of the differences between models are smaller than the spread between seeds, so we cannot rank models that are within about a point of each other.

There are three clear next steps. Firstly, we would extend the data past 2006. This would let us include Item 1A and the financial crisis. Secondly, we would fine-tune the encoder jointly on more test years and seeds \placeholder{update once 05b is done}. Finally, we would add other text, such as 8-K filings or earnings-call transcripts. This would give the models more to work with, and would also test our scaling estimates.

\subsection{Does the text help?}

Yes, but only a little, and only when it is used in the right way. This means fitting the text and last year's volatility together, and only using the text within each year. When we do this, the text lowers the MSE by around 4\% to 5\% on the paper window and 2\% to 2.6\% on the strict window, compared to the straight line. All of this comes from ranking companies within each year. The model used made little difference, but how the text was represented did. FinBERT clearly beat word counts, and the FinBERT pre-trained on 10-Ks beat the one tuned for sentiment.
